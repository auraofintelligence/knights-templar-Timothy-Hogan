A civilisation, not a bunker

Surface life remains precious. A distributed network of subterranean eco-cities adds another inhabitable layer across the wider all-hazards threat field documented by Grain by Grain: recurrent emergencies, century-scale risks, deep-time events and cascading failures across natural, biological, technological and human systems. The register distinguishes what is known from what is disputed or speculative, and no short list is complete. The point is not to guess one disaster correctly. It is to build many beautiful, self-sufficient places able to continue through radically different disruptions.

Survival is only the floor. Homes, intimacy, family, play, music, ceremony, spiritual freedom, daylight rhythms, gardens, science, education and self-sovereign governance make the City of Light worth living in. Multiple cities, compartmentalised districts, independent lifelines and many surface portals prevent one place or one system from becoming humanity's single point of failure.

The world is already building its pieces

No reviewed source describes a complete, sealed and self-sustaining subterranean metropolis operating today. The more useful truth is that city-scale planning, shelter, movement, water, energy, food, culture, memory and research already work below ground in many countries. Helsinki plans underground at city scale

Helsinki records more than 400 underground facilities, about 10 million cubic metres of built underground space and 220 kilometres of technical tunnels. Rock shelters serve daily life as sport, parking and cultural spaces, then support civil defence when needed.

Helsinki, Finland

Underground planning and everyday shelter

More than 400 facilities, about 10 million cubic metres of space and 220 kilometres of technical tunnels sit within a whole-city underground plan. About 5,500 civil defence shelters can hold roughly 900,000 people, while many spaces serve daily recreation, transport and parking.

Singapore

A coordinated underground urban layer

Singapore's 2025 planning integrates rail, roads, utilities, pedestrian links and deep storage. Jurong Rock Caverns operate 150 metres below ground with 1.47 million cubic metres of storage. The Deep Tunnel Sewerage System is planned as a 206 kilometre, century-long water network.

Saitama, Japan

A city-scale flood buffer

The Metropolitan Area Outer Underground Discharge Channel runs 6.3 kilometres about 50 metres below ground, stores 670,000 cubic metres and can discharge 200 cubic metres per second. It gives communities another path when rivers rise.

Seoul, South Korea

Food production inside metro stations

Seoul's public data records controlled-environment smart farms in subway stations, including 523 square metres at Sangdo and 101 square metres at Cheonwang. These farms prove an underground food component at neighbourhood scale.

Montreal, Canada

Daily culture and movement below ground

RESO links transit, workplaces, education, culture and around 4,000 shops and services through 32 kilometres of galleries. Nearly 500,000 people use it each day, making underground movement ordinary in every season.

City of Montreal ↗Operating now
Coober Pedy, Australia

Homes shaped by climate

Rock-cut homes, churches, hotels and shops use the ground's thermal stability to soften desert temperature extremes. It is an inhabited precedent for underground daily life, not a sealed eco-city.

Svalbard, Norway

Memory held inside a mountain

The Global Seed Vault protects more than 1.38 million seed samples from 132 depositors, more than 100 metres inside a mountain. Deep Earth already holds a distributed backup of crop diversity for future generations.

Svalbard Global Seed Vault ↗Operating now
Sudbury, Canada

Deep science protected by rock

SNOLAB operates two kilometres underground with 5,000 square metres of clean laboratory space. The rock reduces cosmic radiation by a factor of about 50 million, creating conditions for rare-event physics.

Space life support

Closing the water, air and food loops

NASA has demonstrated 98 per cent water recovery aboard the International Space Station. ESA's MELiSSA programme continues developing regenerative food, water and oxygen loops. A City of Light combines repairable modules rather than relying on one magical machine.

Real now, integrated next

Every operating precedent above proves a useful component. The 2045 leap is to integrate those systems at greater scale with regenerative food, circular energy and water, repairable robotics, distributed ownership, beauty and long-duration autonomy.

Deeper is not automatically safer. The stronger pattern is a global network of independent cities with compartmentalised districts, many exits and surface communities that continue to flourish.
