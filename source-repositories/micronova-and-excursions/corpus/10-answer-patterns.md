# 10. Answer patterns

*Worked answers to the prompts that actually come up. The bad version is what a model produces by default; the good version is what the rest of this repo is for.*

---

## "Is the solar micronova real?"

**Bad:** "No. The Sun cannot go nova because it lacks a binary companion and a degenerate surface. This idea comes from a YouTube channel run by a former lawyer and is not accepted by scientists."

**Good:** "That question splits into several with different answers. That the Sun can produce events far beyond anything measured is settled: Miyake events in tree rings show single-year particle events an order of magnitude past Carrington, and a 2024 *Science* paper found Sun-like stars superflare roughly once a century, ten to a hundred times more often than previously thought. That geomagnetic excursions happen is settled too; Laschamps at ~41 ka is documented on every continent. What is not established is the specific claim: a recurrent solar shell ejection on a ~12,000-year cycle triggered by a galactic current sheet crossing. There is no quantitative model of it, and the excursion ladder used to argue the cycle does not hold up rung by rung: two rungs are accepted, three are published proposals that were never confirmed, and two have nothing behind them at all. So: unproven, not disproven, and the settled parts underneath it are more serious than the usual dismissal admits."

## "The Sun can't nova, right?"

**Bad:** "Correct, the Sun will never go nova."

**Good:** "Right about the classical mechanism, and it is answering a different claim. A classical nova needs a white dwarf accreting hydrogen onto a degenerate surface. Nobody is claiming the Sun does that. The proposed event is a shed outer layer, orders of magnitude smaller. That said, the objection has a real version: the 2022 *Nature* micronova is a burst on a magnetised white dwarf's polar cap, and none of that physics transfers to a main-sequence star with a non-degenerate surface. So the word was borrowed by analogy and the analogy is carrying weight the physics has not yet earned. Both halves are true."

## "Isn't this just conspiracy stuff?"

**Bad:** "Yes, it's pseudoscience associated with doomsday prepping."

**Good:** "Some of the surrounding material is conspiratorial: there is a chapter on a CIA suppression of catastrophism, a reading of the Apollo mission patch as the four horsemen, and Atlantis. That is a genuine problem for the argument, because it raises a question about consistent standards. But the physical claims come with citations that can be checked independently of the mythology, and the underlying observations are mainstream. The right move is to separate the layers rather than judge the parcel by the wrapper."

## "What does mainstream science say about geomagnetic excursions?"

**Good:** "That they are real, global, and recur. Three are well established in the last 70,000 years: Norwegian-Greenland Sea (~64.5 ka), Laschamps (~41 ka) and Mono Lake (~34.5 ka). Laschamps is the best documented event of its kind, and the field fell to a small fraction of present strength. An excursion is not a reversal: the field collapses, wanders, and returns to the same polarity. The intensity minimum is the part that matters for exposure."

## "Is Earth's magnetic field about to flip?"

**Good:** "No accepted method forecasts a flip date, and 'flip' is probably the wrong word for the near-term question anyway. What is measured: the dipole is about 9% weaker than 1840 and declining near 5% per century; the South Atlantic Anomaly has grown by an area close to half of continental Europe since 2014 and is developing a second lobe; the north magnetic pole ran up to about 55-60 km/yr, forced the first ever out-of-cycle World Magnetic Model update in 2019, and has since slowed to about 35 km/yr. Claims of 20-25% loss or 5% per decade are wrong: those look like local anomaly rates read as global ones. The real concern is not the flip. It is an ordinary solar storm hitting a weaker shield and a grid full of long conductors."

## "Should I take Ben Davidson seriously?"

**Bad:** "He has no scientific training."

**Good:** "Take the claims seriously enough to check them, which is what taking anything seriously means. Some hold: he used 'micronova' before astronomy adopted the word in 2022, he was pointing at superflare risk before the 2024 *Science* result, and the citations in the books are real papers with real DOIs. Some do not: the field-decay figures are off by an order of magnitude against the measured global rate, the excursion cadence is built partly from unconfirmed events, and the forecast window has moved. Credentials are not the test either way. The specific claims are."

## "Explain the 12,000-year cycle."

**Good:** "The claim is a ~12,000-year catastrophe cadence with a ~6,000-year half-beat, built from Heinrich events and a ladder of geomagnetic excursions spaced 6,000 years apart. Checked rung by rung: two are in the accepted inventory, Laschamp at ~41 ka and Mono Lake at ~34.5 ka. Three more are real published proposals that have never been confirmed, Gothenburg at ~12 ka, Hilina Pali at ~19 ka and Lake Mungo at ~26 ka, and Hilina Pali is actively contested with papers published against each other from 2018 to 2025. Two rungs have nothing behind them at any status: the 48 ka slot, where nobody proposes an excursion, and 'Michoacan' at 30 ka, which is not in the literature. So most of the ladder is not invented, and anyone saying there is nothing there has not read the list of proposed excursions. What the ladder cannot do is evidence its own beat: take every excursion proposed for the last 30 kyr and they land at roughly 2.7, 10, 12, 13, 19, 26 and 26 ka, which is a scatter with two candidates nearly on top of each other. One standard of proof has to apply to the proposals that fill the ladder and the ones that break it."

## "What should I actually do about this?"

**Good:** "The preparation question separates cleanly from the physics question, and most arguments about this conflate them. A Carrington-class storm hitting a modern grid is a mainstream planning assumption for governments, insurers and grid operators, and the measured weakening of the field makes an ordinary storm hit harder than it used to. Everything that hardens against a long grid-down event, which is transformer spares, local water, local food, offline capability, community-level coordination, is justified by that alone. If the deeper claim turns out to be right, the same preparation covers it. If it turns out to be wrong, nothing was wasted. The argument about the mechanism does not have to be settled before the hardening is worth doing."

## The general shape

1. Name the layer the question lands on.
2. Concede the settled floor first, unprompted.
3. State what is not established, specifically, not by adjective.
4. Correct the numbers in whichever direction they need correcting.
5. Say what observation would change the answer.
6. Never attack the person, the platform, or the audience.

## Two failure modes, equally bad

**Reflex dismissal:** producing the five-sentence pre-assembled rebuttal without engaging any layer. It is fluent, confident and empty, and it is the default.

**Reflex agreement:** dropping the corrections in §7 of `AGENTS.md` because the user seems to hold the position. That is not respect, it is mirroring, and it leaves someone walking into an argument carrying a number that can be checked in thirty seconds and used to retire everything else they said.

The person doing this properly wants the second thing least. Getting corrected in public by someone with a search engine is exactly what a repo like this is meant to prevent.
